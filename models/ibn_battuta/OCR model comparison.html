Arabic OCR comparison · Ibn Battuta in Egypt, 5 pages · 27 July 2026

Which model is actually best?

Eight outputs scored against a hand-made transcript of the five source pages (538 words, 355 distinct). Five indicators as requested — accuracy, hallucination, English errors, malformed Arabic, and mixed-script contamination — plus numeric fidelity and right-to-left layout, which turned out to separate the models more sharply than anything else.

The answer, in two parts

On your five indicators, Baseer OCR wins — clearly

It leads or ties on every one: highest word accuracy, lowest rate of malformed Arabic, zero English contamination, zero mixed-script tokens, the only model to get all six printed numbers right, and the only one of eight that read the two-column right-to-left page in the correct order. Composite score 97.5 / 100, ahead of LightOnOCR.

But the indicators miss the thing that should decide this. Word-level metrics can only compare against words that are on the page. They cannot detect text a model invents wholesale in a region it failed to read — and that is exactly what the winner does.

What the numbers say

Baseer OCR produces the cleanest, most faithful Arabic prose of any model tested. For the body text of a document, it is genuinely the best of these eight.

What the numbers miss

Faced with a publisher block it could not read, it invented a complete one: an Amman address, a telephone number, an e-mail and a website. All fluent. All false. The real imprint is in Alexandria.

The distinction that matters most

A model that garbles is safer than a model that invents. When FastOCR hit the same unreadable publisher block, it emitted obvious fragments — visibly broken, easy to catch, easy to route for manual review. When Baseer and Datalab hit it, they produced clean, plausible, professional-looking text that was entirely fabricated.

Nothing downstream will flag an invented address or a fake ISBN. It reads correctly, passes validation, and propagates. If your pipeline extracts structured fields — addresses, identifiers, contact details, amounts — fabrication is disqualifying in a way that a low accuracy score is not.

Recommendation

For clean Arabic body text, use Baseer OCR. It is measurably the best transcriber here, and the only one that respects right-to-left column order.

Do not let it touch structured fields unreviewed. Addresses, identifiers, contact details and reference numbers need either human verification or a second model to cross-check. Where two models disagree on a structured field, treat both as unreliable for that field.

Rule out GLM-OCR and PaddleOCR-VL entirely. GLM returned a document unrelated to the input. PaddleOCR got all six printed numbers wrong while producing numbers that look right — the worst possible failure for anything numeric.

Then test Mistral OCR 4 and dots.mocr. Neither is in this run, and both have published multilingual results stronger than anything here. Until they are tested on these same pages, the comparison is incomplete.

Method

Reference. A transcript of all five pages made by reading the page images directly — 538 words, 355 distinct. No vendor transcript was used.

Accuracy is the share of reference words recovered, matched as a multiset so repetition counts. Bad Arabic is the share of output words absent from the source; a few will be transcription slips on my side rather than model errors, so treat small differences as noise. English injected counts Latin fragments sitting inside Arabic running text, and deliberately excludes standalone English image captions, which are a separate issue. Layout checks page 2, where correct right-to-left output places the main column (فتى من طنجة) before the sidebar (مدينة طنجة).

Caveat. Five pages of one document. Enough to disqualify a model; not enough to certify one. The winner here earned a larger trial, not a purchase order.
